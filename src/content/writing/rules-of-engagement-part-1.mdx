*Part one of two.*

I am reading *The American Hagwon* by Min Jee Lin, and I did not get far before I had to stop. The book opens with one short line: "The rules of engagement have changed already." I read it twice and put the book down. It gave a name to something I had been feeling for months but could not quite say.

For most of my life, a few things did the talking for us. A good degree said you were capable. A good employer said you were safe. A job title said you could be trusted. If you did your work well and kept your head down, the system would notice you sooner or later. I am not sure when it stopped being true. I think it happened slowly and then suddenly, all at once.

The first place I saw it was in how people find jobs. When I started my career, you looked for a vacancy, sent your CV, and waited. Today the better opportunities seem to come the other way around. Someone sees your work, reads what you share, or knows you through a community, and they reach out before a role is ever posted. Senior people were always hired like this. What has changed is that now everyone is expected to work this way.

Once I started noticing it, I saw it everywhere. Even the most powerful people are not exempt. A mayor or a prime minister can no longer just do the job; they also have to show the job, every single day, on social media. Politicians always cared about their image, but there used to be a newspaper or a TV channel in the middle. Now there is nothing in between, and the ones who cannot keep up quietly fall behind.

Education tells the same story, and this one feels personal. In India, a good degree was the ladder. It led to a software job, and for many of us that job led to a career abroad. That ladder feels shaky now, weakened by AI on one side and geopolitics on the other. A degree from a top institute still opens doors, but a student from a smaller college now needs something more, a portfolio or a body of work that speaks for them. The degree has become the entry ticket rather than the finish line.

Work itself has changed too. During COVID, companies hired fast and then let people go just as fast. The AI boom brought another wave of hiring, and now, as AI makes teams more productive, the layoffs come in the thousands. The old promise of loyalty in exchange for security has quietly disappeared. Whether our contracts say so or not, many of us are now a one-person business.

It is not only office work, either. Publishers ask new authors how many followers they have before they ask about the book. Young athletes earn money from their personal brand, so the one with the bigger audience can out-earn a more talented teammate. What unsettles me most is that the old gatekeepers did not really disappear. New ones simply took their place. Platforms now decide who gets seen, and slowly that changes what people choose to do. If I have one free hour, I have to ask myself honestly whether I spend it going deeper in my field or making something I can post. And even the platforms keep moving. Websites spent twenty years learning how to rank on Google, and now they are learning how to show up in AI answers. The rules did not change once. They keep changing.

When I think about who struggles in this new world, I don't think it is people without talent. It is people without a community. They did everything they were told. They got the degree, stayed loyal, and kept their heads down. Nobody told them the game had changed.

Maybe that is why the word "already" in that first line stayed with me. The rules have already changed, and most of us were the last to find out.

What we can do about it is a question for part two.
